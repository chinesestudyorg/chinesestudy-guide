# Using Pinyin and Translation to Read Classical Chinese

[Home](../README.md) · [Getting started](getting-started.md)

The original text, pronunciation support, translation, and notes answer different questions. Keeping those questions separate makes it easier to see what you understand and what still needs attention.

## Pinyin helps with pronunciation

Use pinyin when an unfamiliar character stops you from reading a line aloud. Once you can follow the line, look back at the characters and notice how they form the phrase.

Knowing how to pronounce a character does not settle its meaning in a classical passage. A familiar character may need a different explanation in this context. Consult the translation and notes for that question.

## A translation gives you a reading to examine

Read a short translated passage, then locate its main ideas in the Chinese. Start with one phrase rather than matching every word. Look for where the translation makes relationships explicit that you found difficult to see in the original.

If you compare another translation, record its source. Differences can help you identify a question about wording or interpretation; they need to be examined in the passage, rather than decided by whichever version sounds smoother.

## Notes add context

Consult the notes after you have identified a specific difficulty. A name, an image, an unfamiliar expression, and the relationship between two sentences may each require a different kind of explanation.

Keep the Chinese phrase beside your note. This makes the explanation useful when you return to the text and prevents your reading record from becoming a collection of detached summaries.

## Try the method on a real passage

Use [Tao Te Ching chapter 1](https://chinesestudy.org/en/books/dao-de-jing/01/) for a compact prose reading, or [Drinking Alone under the Moon](https://chinesestudy.org/en/books/tangshi-sanbai/004-yue-xia-du-zhuo/) for poetry. Work through one phrase using the sequence above, then reread the whole passage.
